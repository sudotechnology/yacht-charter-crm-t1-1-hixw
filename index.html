Yacht Charter CRM: A Beginner's Guide

If you sell or manage yacht charters, you already run a customer system. It might be an inbox, a spreadsheet of client preferences, a shared calendar of options, and a notebook of which captain a family liked three summers ago. A CRM (customer relationship management system) pulls that scattered information into one place so you can answer an enquiry faster, follow up at the right moment, and remember what matters to each guest. This guide answers the questions people ask most when they first look at CRM software for the charter business: what it actually does, how it differs from the tools you already use, and whether a small brokerage or fleet really needs one.

What is a yacht charter CRM?

A CRM is a database of people and the conversations you have with them, organised around a sales process. In a general business that process might be lead, quote, invoice. In yacht charter it is more specific: enquiry, qualification (dates, cruising area, group size, budget), yacht shortlist, option or hold, contract, deposit, balance, preference sheet, the charter itself, and then the follow-up that hopefully turns into a repeat booking.

A CRM built for this trade understands those stages. Instead of forcing you to describe a week in the Cyclades as a generic 'deal', it lets you record the yacht, embarkation and disembarkation ports, charter dates, the contract type, the advance provisioning allowance on crewed charters, and the commission arrangement with the central agent or owner. That structure is what separates a charter-focused tool from a general sales platform you would have to customise heavily.

Who uses one?

Retail charter brokers are the most common users. They handle a high volume of enquiries, many of which never book, and the ones that do often book again years later. Remembering a client's dietary needs, the children's ages, and the fact that they disliked a long passage on day two is exactly the kind of detail a CRM keeps safe.

Central agents and management companies use CRM features to track relationships with brokers as well as end clients: who sent which enquiry, which brokers book which yachts, and where an option stands. Bareboat and crewed fleet operators use them alongside their booking or reservation systems to manage direct clients, agent partners, and returning skippers. Even a solo broker working from a laptop benefits once the client list grows beyond what memory and an inbox can handle.

How is it different from a booking system or a spreadsheet?

A booking or reservation system is built around the yacht: availability, pricing, base, extras, and check-in logistics. A CRM is built around the person: who they are, how they found you, what they have asked for, and what you promised to send next. Many operators need both, and the useful question is how well they share information rather than which one replaces the other.

A spreadsheet works for a while, and plenty of brokers start there. The problems appear when two people edit it, when you need to see every email exchanged with a client alongside their record, or when you want a reminder to chase a contract that has been sitting unsigned for five days. Spreadsheets also make it hard to see your pipeline at a glance, for example how many options expire this week or how much commission is waiting on balance payments.

Do I actually need one?

A few signs suggest you have outgrown improvised tools. You lose track of options and discover a hold has lapsed only when the central agent tells you. You cannot easily find past clients to contact before the next season. Colleagues ask each other where a contract or preference sheet is. Follow-ups depend on whoever happens to remember them.

If none of that sounds familiar and you handle a handful of charters a year, a careful spreadsheet and a disciplined inbox may still be enough. If several of those points hit home, a purpose-built yacht charter crm is likely to save time each week and reduce the small mistakes that cost bookings and goodwill.

What to check before choosing

Start with your own process. Write down, step by step, how an enquiry becomes a completed charter in your business, including who does each step. Then test each shortlisted tool against that list using a real recent booking, not the vendor's demo data.

Look closely at email handling, because most charter communication still happens by email. Check how the tool records options and their expiry dates, whether it can hold yacht details without endless manual typing, how it tracks payments and commission, and what happens to your data if you leave. Ask about access from a phone, since much of this work happens at boat shows, on a dock, or while travelling. Finally, consider who will keep the records tidy: a CRM is only as useful as the information people put into it.

FAQ

Is a yacht charter CRM only for large brokerages?
No. Solo brokers often benefit early because they have no colleague to cover follow-ups or remember client details. The value comes from organisation and reminders, not from team size.
Can I use a general CRM instead?
You can, but expect to build custom fields for yachts, charter dates, ports, options, contract types and commission. Some teams manage this well; others find the customisation never quite fits and drift back to spreadsheets.
Will a CRM replace my booking or availability system?
Usually not. Booking systems manage yachts and availability; a CRM manages clients and the sales process. The goal is for the two to work side by side with as little double entry as possible.
When is the best time to start using one?
Ideally in the quieter months before the main booking period, so you can import data, test your process, and train anyone involved before enquiries peak.
