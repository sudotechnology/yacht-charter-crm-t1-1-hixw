Getting Started with a Yacht Charter CRM

Choosing a CRM is the easy part. Getting it working, with clean data and a team that actually uses it, is where most of the effort goes. The good news is that a charter business has a fairly predictable rhythm, which makes planning a rollout straightforward if you start at the right time. This page covers how to pick a system, prepare your data, run a sensible trial, and introduce the new tool without disrupting live bookings.

Map your process before you look at software

Before booking any demos, write out how a charter moves through your business. Note every stage from enquiry to post-charter follow-up, who is responsible for each step, what documents are created, and where information is currently stored. Include the awkward parts: how you handle options with central agents, how you chase signatures, how preference sheets reach the crew.

This exercise does two things. It gives you a checklist to test each product against, and it often reveals steps that are inconsistent between colleagues. Agreeing on one way of working before you configure a CRM is far easier than trying to fix inconsistent habits afterwards.

Shortlisting and running a fair trial

Narrow your options to two or three tools. Use any free trial with real data: take a handful of recent bookings, ideally including a crewed charter, a bareboat charter and an enquiry that did not convert, and run each through the system from start to finish. Note where you had to work around the software or type the same information twice.

Ask vendors direct questions. How is data exported if you leave, and in what format? Where is data hosted, and how is client privacy handled? What does support look like during your busy season, when time zones may differ? How are prices structured as you add users? Clear answers to these questions are often more telling than a polished demonstration.

Cleaning and importing your data

Most businesses import contacts from spreadsheets, email accounts or an older system. Before you do, clean the data. Remove duplicates, standardise how names, countries and phone numbers are written, and decide which fields really matter. Importing messy data simply moves the mess into a more expensive place.

Be selective about history. Past charter records for repeat clients are valuable and worth importing carefully. Years of old enquiries that never progressed may be better archived than imported. Also check you have a lawful basis to keep and contact people on your list, especially if you plan to send marketing emails from the new system.

Timing and rolling it out to your team

In most markets the quieter months after the main season are the best time to switch, before the next wave of enquiries and boat-show leads. Avoid changing systems in the middle of peak booking or while many charters are underway, when every minute goes to live clients.

Start with a small group or a single workflow, such as new enquiries only, then expand once it runs smoothly. Write a short internal guide covering what must be recorded and where, and agree basic rules, for example that every enquiry is logged on the same day and every option has an expiry date entered. If you are still comparing products, a purpose-built option such as the one linked on this site's home page is a reasonable place to start your shortlist.

Common beginner mistakes

Over-customising is the most frequent problem. It is tempting to add dozens of fields and automations on day one. Start lean, use the system for a few weeks, then add only what people genuinely miss.

Other mistakes include running the old spreadsheet in parallel indefinitely, which splits information across two places; leaving records half-complete so nobody trusts them; and relying on automated emails that feel impersonal to high-value clients. Finally, do not forget the post-charter stage. Recording how the trip went and when to get back in touch is where much of the long-term value of a CRM lies, because repeat and referral business is so important in this trade.

FAQ

How long does setup usually take?
It depends on the size of your data and how much you customise. A solo broker with a tidy contact list can be working within days; a team migrating years of records should plan for several weeks, including testing and training.
Should I keep my spreadsheet as a backup?
Keep an archived copy for reference, but stop updating it once the CRM is live. Maintaining both leads to conflicting information and undermines trust in the new system.
What if my team resists using it?
Involve them in mapping the process and testing tools, keep the first version simple, and show quick wins such as automatic email history and option reminders. Clear rules about what must be logged also help.
Can I switch CRM later if it does not work out?
Yes, provided you can export your data in a usable format. Confirm export options before signing up so you are never locked in.
