Which Yacht Charter CRM Features Actually Matter?

Feature lists for CRM software tend to be long, and they rarely distinguish between what you will use every day and what you will open once a year. For charter work, a small number of functions do most of the heavy lifting. This page walks through them in the order a booking usually travels, from first enquiry to the message you send after guests disembark, and explains what to look for in each so you can compare tools sensibly.

Enquiry capture and qualification

Enquiries arrive by website form, email, phone, broker partners and boat shows. A good system lets you log each one quickly, ideally pulling website forms in automatically, and record the essentials: preferred dates and flexibility, cruising area, number of guests and cabins, crewed or bareboat, budget range, and how the client found you.

Qualification fields matter because they shape everything that follows. If you know a group is six adults and two children wanting a crewed catamaran in Croatia in July, you can shortlist accurately and avoid sending irrelevant proposals. Look for a tool that makes these fields easy to fill in on a busy day, since half-completed records are the most common reason CRMs fail.

Client profiles and preferences

The client record is the heart of the system. Beyond contact details it should hold past charters, the yachts and crews involved, notes from conversations, and the preference information that makes repeat business possible: dietary requirements, allergies, celebration dates, water toys they enjoyed, and any problems on previous trips.

Check how the tool links people together. Charter parties often include a lead client who signs and pays, a personal assistant who handles logistics, and guests whose details you need for crew lists. Being able to see those relationships, and to know which person to contact about what, saves confusion later. Also check privacy controls, because you are storing personal and sometimes sensitive information about guests.

Yacht shortlists, options and holds

Most brokers send a shortlist of yachts before anything is booked. A charter-focused CRM should let you attach yachts to an enquiry and generate a proposal without retyping specifications each time. Some tools connect to external yacht databases or fleet booking platforms; if that matters to you, confirm exactly which sources are supported and how often data updates.

Options are where money is commonly lost. When you place a hold on a yacht for a client, it has an expiry date, and missing it can mean the yacht goes to another broker. Look for clear option tracking with reminders before expiry, plus a view of all open options so nothing sits forgotten in an email thread.

Contracts, payments and commission

Once a client commits, the record should move to a contract stage and track the documents involved. For crewed charters many brokers work with standard industry contract forms, while bareboat operators often use their own terms. Either way, you want to see at a glance whether a contract has been sent, signed by each party, and filed.

Payment tracking is equally important. Charters are often paid in instalments, typically a deposit on signing and a balance before embarkation, with the advance provisioning allowance handled separately on crewed yachts. A useful CRM shows what is due, what has been received, and what is overdue. If you are a broker, commission tracking tells you what you are owed and when, which helps with cash flow planning during the off-season.

Communication, tasks and reminders

Email integration is close to essential. Being able to see every message to and from a client on their record, without copying and pasting, is one of the biggest time savers a CRM offers. Templates help for repeated messages such as proposal cover notes, contract reminders and preference sheet requests, as long as you still personalise them.

Task reminders keep the process moving: chase an unsigned contract, request the preference sheet a set number of weeks before embarkation, confirm transfers, send a welcome-home message after the charter. Automated sequences can handle some of this, but use them carefully. Charter clients expect a personal service, and an obviously automated message at the wrong moment undoes a lot of good work.

Reporting and nice-to-have extras

Basic reporting should answer practical questions: how many enquiries came in this month and from where, how many turned into bookings, which destinations and yacht types are in demand, and what revenue and commission are confirmed for the season. You do not need complex dashboards to start; a few reliable figures reviewed regularly are more useful.

Extras such as client portals, e-signature, marketing newsletters and mobile apps can be valuable, but judge them against your actual workflow. A feature you will not use adds clutter and sometimes cost. FAQ

What is the single most important feature for a broker?
For most brokers it is reliable option and follow-up tracking combined with email history on each client record. Those two together prevent the most common lost bookings.
Do I need e-signature built into the CRM?
Not necessarily. Many businesses use a separate signing service. What matters is that the CRM shows the contract status clearly so you know who still needs to sign.
Should a CRM handle accounting?
Usually it tracks what is due and received, while your accounting software handles bookkeeping, tax and invoicing records. Check whether the two can exchange data to avoid entering payments twice.
How important is mobile access?
Quite important in this trade. Brokers and operators often work at boat shows, marinas and while travelling, so being able to look up a client or log an enquiry from a phone is a real advantage.
Can a CRM track charters across different cruising areas?
A charter-focused one should let you record destination, ports and dates for each booking, which also lets you see where demand is coming from over time.
